# Choosing where to submit

Your first submission decision matters more than your template. Pick the wrong venue and even a strong paper gets desk-rejected, reviewed by the wrong people, or lost in a journal nobody in your field reads.

## Fit beats prestige

The instinct is to aim for the most famous venue you have heard of. Resist it. For a first paper, the question is not "how prestigious is this venue" but "do the people who care about my problem read this venue". A solid paper at a well-matched mid-tier venue gets cited, discussed, and builds your record. The same paper at a mismatched top venue gets rejected with reviews that say "interesting, but not for us".

A quick sanity check: open your own bibliography. Where were the ten papers you cite most published? If half of them appeared at the same two or three venues, you have found your shortlist.

## Reading a call for papers

A call for papers (CFP) is a contract. Read it like one, and look for these five things:

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Scope and topics | Desk rejection risk if your topic is not listed |
| Page limit | Determines how much you can say, see [page limits](/learn/page-limits-without-crimes/) |
| Deadline and timezone | AoE (Anywhere on Earth) is common, but not universal |
| Review model | Double-blind changes how you write, see [anonymization](/learn/anonymization-double-blind/) |
| Format requirements | Which class file, which citation style |

The review model deserves special attention. Conferences typically give you one shot with a fixed decision date. Journals allow revision rounds but take longer. The tradeoffs are covered in [journals vs conferences](/learn/journals-vs-conferences/).

## Workshops and short papers: the on-ramp

Nobody says your first paper must be a full conference paper. Workshops attached to major conferences accept 4 to 6 page papers, have friendlier review, and put you in a room with people working on your topic. Short paper and poster tracks work similarly. Many strong publication records started with a workshop paper that later grew into the full version.

If you are still mapping out the whole process, the [first paper roadmap](/learn/first-paper-roadmap/) walks through it end to end.

## Predatory journals: the red flags

Some "journals" exist only to collect publication fees. Warning signs:

- Unsolicited email inviting you to submit, often flattering a paper you posted as a preprint
- Promised review turnaround of days, not months
- A scope covering wildly unrelated fields ("International Journal of Science, Engineering and Management")
- Editorial board members you cannot verify, or who do not know they are on it
- Fees mentioned only after acceptance

When in doubt, ask your advisor or check whether the journal is indexed where your field expects (DBLP, PubMed, Web of Science). If a venue would embarrass you on a CV in five years, it is not a shortcut, it is a liability.

## Then match the format

Once you have a venue, its formatting rules follow: IEEE venues want [IEEEtran](/learn/ieee-format-in-practice/), ACM venues want [acmart](/learn/acm-format-in-practice/), Springer proceedings want [llncs](/learn/springer-elsevier-formats/). The venue chooses the format. You just have to compile it.
