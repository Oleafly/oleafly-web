# The deadline lifecycle

A call for papers looks like a wall of dates. Once you can read it, it is a map of the next four months of your life. Here is the decoder ring.

## The dates, in order

| Date | What it means |
|---|---|
| Abstract registration | You submit title, abstract, and authors. Reviewers get assigned based on this. |
| Full paper deadline | The actual PDF is due. Usually about a week after abstract registration. |
| Notification | Accept or reject lands in your inbox. Often with a rebuttal round in between. |
| Rebuttal window | A few days to respond to reviews before final decisions. |
| Camera-ready | The final, polished version for the proceedings. |

The abstract deadline is not optional at most venues. Miss it and you cannot submit the paper at all, even if the PDF is ready. Register early with a placeholder abstract if you must, then refine it. Our guide to [writing an abstract](/learn/write-an-abstract/) helps here.

## What AoE actually means

Most conference deadlines are AoE: Anywhere on Earth, which is UTC-12. The deadline has not passed until it is past midnight in the last timezone on the planet, an uninhabited stretch of the Pacific.

Concrete example: a deadline of "May 15, AoE" means 11:59 PM on May 15 in UTC-12. If you are in Berlin (UTC+2), that is 1:59 PM on May 16. In California (UTC-7), it is 4:59 AM on May 16. So yes, you often get a bonus morning. No, you should not plan around it.

## Why people track deadlines obsessively

Conference deadlines are hard walls. There is no "sorry, five minutes late" at a top venue, and the next submission window might be six months away. That is why researchers keep deadline trackers open in a pinned tab. If you work in Oleafly, there is a conference deadlines browser built in, backed by the open ccf-deadlines dataset, so you can check dates without leaving your draft. Always confirm against the official call for papers, since dates shift.

Choosing which deadline to aim for is its own decision. See [choosing your venue](/learn/choose-your-venue/).

## Plan backwards from the deadline

Work back from the full paper deadline:

- [ ] Deadline minus 1 day: buffer. Something will go wrong. This day absorbs it.
- [ ] Minus 3 days: freeze content. Only fix typos and figures.
- [ ] Minus 1 week: full draft done, sent to co-authors or a friendly reader.
- [ ] Minus 2 weeks: all experiments finished, results tables locked.
- [ ] Minus 4 weeks: writing in earnest, figures drafted.

The single most common first-timer mistake is treating the deadline as when you finish writing. Treat it as when you finish fixing.

## After you submit

The lifecycle keeps going. Reviews arrive, and [peer review](/learn/peer-review-explained/) makes a lot more sense once you know what reviewers are asked to do. Then comes the rebuttal, the decision, and if things go well, the camera-ready sprint. Each of those has its own lesson in this track.

One deadline at a time. That is the whole trick.
